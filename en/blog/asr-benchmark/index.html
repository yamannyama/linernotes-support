Choosing a speech recognizer by benchmark alone is risky. Measure with the audio your app actually handles

November 6, 2026 · Liner Notes · 日本語版

When I re-evaluated the speech recognizer for my iPhone diary app, Apple's recognizer looked best on the commonly used read-speech benchmark. When I re-measured on conversational audio, which is what the app actually handles, Apple's character error rate was about 1.8× that of Parakeet, the model I was already using. Had I switched based on the benchmark, transcription quality would have dropped. Here is how I compared them, where I almost went wrong, and what I chose, with the numbers.

What I wanted to do

I build Liner Notes, an iPhone diary app that turns conversations into text on the device. For transcription I was using NVIDIA's Parakeet Japanese model through FluidAudio on Core ML.

In October 2026, Apple's recognizer (iOS SpeechTranscriber), Mistral's Voxtral and IBM's Granite Speech all became usable for Japanese, so I compared them to see whether switching was worth it. The requirements: it runs on the iPhone, it is fast enough to process in the background after recording, and it transcribes conversation accurately.

How Japanese speech recognition is evaluated

Japanese has no spaces between words, so instead of word error rate (WER) the usual measure is character error rate (CER). You align the reference and the output character by character and divide substitutions, deletions and insertions by the number of reference characters. I normalized both with NFKC and removed punctuation, spaces and symbols before comparing. Notation differences such as 三十分 vs 30分 still count as errors.

Public Japanese evaluation sets fall roughly into read speech and spontaneous or conversational speech.

Read speech first, and I was ready to switch to Apple

I started with FLEURS Japanese (dev set, 266 clips, 56.5 minutes). Voxtral's published results use the same data, so it seemed easy to compare.

All models ran on the same Mac (Apple silicon, 24 GB).

  • Parakeet: FluidAudio 0.17.4. As in the app, segments are cut at quiet points every 6–12 seconds and padded with 0.5 s of silence on each side
  • Apple: SpeechTranscriber (ja-JP)
  • Voxtral: 6-bit quantized Voxtral Mini 4B Realtime on the Mac GPU, at two latencies: 480 ms (default) and 2400 ms (most accurate)

Voxtral at 2400 ms was the most accurate, but about 110× slower than Parakeet and 3.6 GB even at 6 bits, so running it in the background on an iPhone is not realistic. Apple made fewer errors than Parakeet, and since it is built into the OS, the app would not need to ship a model. At this point I was planning to switch to Apple.

Measured on conversation, Apple's error rate was about 1.8×

FLEURS, though, is clear reading in a quiet room. What the app actually handles is conversation, with hesitations, backchannels and overlapping speech. So I measured again under the same conditions on the ReazonSpeech test set, which is TV broadcast conversation (2,632 clips, 258 minutes).

ModelCERClips with no outputTime for 258 min
Parakeet12.74%0118 s
Apple22.96%37631 s

The ranking flipped. Looking at Apple's errors, it returned nothing for 37 short clips and sometimes added polite phrases nobody said, such as "thank you very much" and "nice to meet you." It is the same kind of error that a beta tester had reported: a "hello" that nobody said showing up in the transcript. A greeting nobody said, left in someone's diary, is a real problem.

Apple's time includes starting up for each clip, so the speed gap is smaller than the table suggests. But for conversation accuracy, Parakeet was the one to choose.

Adding Granite: 0.5 s of silence moved the number by 3 points

Next I added IBM's Granite Speech 5.0 TurboCTC (470M parameters), which gained Japanese support on October 21. The first result on conversation was Granite 9.79% vs Parakeet 12.74%. Now it looked like I should switch to Granite.

But Parakeet's clips were padded with 0.5 s of silence on each side, as in the app, and Granite's were not. Here are the results with matched conditions (Granite converted to Core ML for the iPhone).

ConditionGraniteParakeet
Conversation (ReazonSpeech), no padding9.99%11.73%
Conversation (ReazonSpeech), 0.5 s padding13.03%12.74%
Continuous audio (about 4 min × 14), 0.5 s padding4.36%4.39%

ReazonSpeech clips contain fragments of the neighboring utterances that are not in the reference captions. Granite does not transcribe fragments at the very edge of its input; with padding they are no longer at the edge, so it transcribes them, and that looks like more errors. On segments cut from long continuous recordings, which is what the app actually handles, there is no difference between the two.

Finally, reading my own recordings side by side

With no difference on public data, I read the two transcripts side by side for 6 of my own recordings (2 h 10 min in total).

What I looked atGraniteParakeet

Backchannels and overlapping speech matter in a record of a conversation. Granite was better on numbers and names, but overall Parakeet missed less.

Conclusion: stay with Parakeet

The app stays on Parakeet. It is the most accurate on conversation and does not drop backchannels. I kept the Core ML conversion of Granite as a fallback in case speed becomes a problem.

Summary

  • Compare speech recognizers on evaluation data close to the audio your app handles. For conversation, use conversational audio such as ReazonSpeech. Read speech (FLEURS) ranked them the other way round
  • Match the preprocessing: how segments are cut and how much silence is padded. 0.5 s of silence moved the error rate by 3 points
  • If public data shows no difference, read transcripts of your own recordings side by side. Missing backchannels and greetings nobody said barely show up in the error rate
